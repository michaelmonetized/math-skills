This is pure **explore**: widen the pool of what could go into an exposition (a paper introduction, a survey, a talk, a blog post) without committing to a structure. Structure is a later skill's job (`writing-shape-theory` or `writing-bettis`).

Run a grilling session (call the Skill tool with "epsilon-delta-grilling") aimed at the user's understanding of the mathematics, not its formal statement, and append fragments to a single Markdown file as they appear, from either side of the conversation. If no path was given, ask once and remember it. Start the file with one H1 working title and nothing else.

## What is a fragment

Anything that might survive into the final text, readable by the author even if not by a cold reader. Fragments are self-similar at every scale: a word, a sentence, a paragraph. For mathematics, the most valuable kinds are:

- **The one-line reason it's true**: the idea you would say at the blackboard, before any epsilons.
- **The motivating example**: the smallest case where the phenomenon is visible.
- **The example that breaks it**: why each hypothesis is there.
- **The picture**: how the author actually visualises the objects.
- **The history**: what was known, what was surprising, who asked the question (with references to verify later).
- **The leading word**: a coinage the whole piece can hang on. When the conversation keeps circling an idea, push to name it.

## Rules

- Append as you go; never batch.
- Do not tidy, reorder, or define terms: that is shaping, not exploring.
- Mark any historical or bibliographic claim `[verify]`; it gets checked with `researxiv` before it enters a draft.
